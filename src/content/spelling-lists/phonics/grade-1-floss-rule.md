## Introduction: what students learn

This lesson teaches the **Floss Rule**. In many one-syllable words with one short vowel, the final **f**, **l**, **s**, and sometimes **z** are doubled.

Students learn that doubled final letters usually make one sound. The second letter is part of the spelling pattern, not an extra sound to pronounce.

## Learning Objectives

Students will learn to:

- identify one-syllable short-vowel words;
- notice final **f**, **l**, **s**, and **z** sounds;
- double the final consonant in common Floss Rule words;
- explain that doubled final consonants usually spell one sound;
- compare Floss Rule words with short-vowel words that do not use the rule.

## Why this spelling concept matters

The Floss Rule helps first graders spell many common short words accurately. Without the rule, students often write *mis* for *miss*, *hil* for *hill*, or *of* for *off*.

This lesson also builds the larger idea that English spelling has patterns students can learn and apply.

## Parent-friendly explanation

In words like *hill*, *miss*, and *puff*, the last sound is not said twice. The word has one final sound, but the spelling uses two letters. That doubled spelling often happens after one short vowel in a one-syllable word.

## The spelling rule or generalization

Use this first-grade generalization:

In a one-syllable word with one short vowel, double final **f**, **l**, **s**, and sometimes **z**.

Examples:

- **ff**: off, puff, cuff
- **ll**: hill, bell, doll
- **ss**: miss, kiss, mess
- **zz**: buzz

## Common mistakes children make

- Spelling *hill* as *hil* because they hear only one /l/ sound.
- Adding double letters to words that do not follow the pattern.
- Thinking the doubled letters should be pronounced twice.
- Forgetting to check whether the vowel is short.

## Common Misconceptions

- **Misconception:** Double letters mean two sounds.  
  **Correction:** In these words, the doubled letters usually spell one final sound.

- **Misconception:** The Floss Rule works for every word ending in f, l, s, or z.  
  **Correction:** It is a useful generalization for many one-syllable short-vowel words, not a rule for every English word.

## How parents can practice at home

Ask your child to say the word, tap the sounds, and identify the short vowel. Then ask, “Does it end with f, l, s, or z?” If yes, have your child try the doubled spelling and read the word back.

Use only a few words at a time. The goal is to build the habit of checking the vowel and final sound.
