A prefix is a word part added to the beginning of a base word to build a new, related word. The base word is a whole word on its own — *kind*, *happy*, *lock*, *read* — and the prefix attaches directly to its front without leaving anything out: *un- + kind = unkind*, *re- + read = reread*.

## How un- and re- change meaning

*Un-* and *re-* are two of the most common prefixes in English, but they do different jobs.

| Prefix | Meaning | Word equation | Whole-word meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| *un-* | not / the opposite of | *un- + happy = unhappy* | not happy |
| *un-* | to reverse an action | *un- + lock = unlock* | to undo locking |
| *re-* | again | *re- + read = reread* | to read again |

Notice that *un-* is doing two different jobs above. On a describing word like *happy* or *fair*, it means "not": *unhappy* is not happy. On an action word like *lock* or *tie*, it means "reverse": *unlock* undoes a lock rather than meaning "not lock." Checking whether the base word describes a state (*happy*, *fair*, *safe*) or names an action (*lock*, *tie*) is the fastest way to tell which job *un-* is doing in a given word. *Re-* is more consistent: it means "again" in nearly every word it appears in, as in *redo*, *replay*, and *retell*.

## What to notice

- Find the base word first, then read the prefix as its own chunk: *un- / happy*, not *unh- / appy*.
- Say what the prefix contributes, then check that it fits the whole word's meaning.
- The base word's spelling does not change when *un-* or *re-* is added — no letters are dropped or doubled inside the base itself.
- A repeated letter can still appear at the boundary when the base word starts with the same letter the prefix ends in, as in *re- + read = reread* and *re- + write = rewrite*. That is simply both letters — one from the prefix, one from the base — sitting next to each other, not an extra spelling rule.

## How a prefix differs from a suffix

A prefix attaches to the front of a base word; a suffix attaches to the end. That difference in position comes with a difference in behavior. Adding *un-* or *re-* never changes how the base word itself is spelled — *happy* stays *happy* inside *unhappy*, and *read* stays *read* inside *reread*. Many suffixes are different: adding one can force a spelling change to the base word, such as doubling a final consonant or dropping a final silent *e* (see [Spelling Rules for Adding Suffixes](/skills/suffix-spelling-changes)). Suffixes can also change a word's job in a sentence, such as turning a describing word into a noun or comparing two things (see [Common Suffixes](/skills/common-suffixes)). Prefixes like *un-* and *re-* don't do that — they change or intensify meaning without changing a word's grammatical role.

## How a prefix differs from a syllable

A syllable is a chunk of a word built around one vowel sound, used for pronunciation. A prefix is a chunk of a word that carries its own meaning. The two can overlap — *un-* in *unhappy* is both a syllable and a prefix — but they don't have to. Many syllables carry no independent meaning at all (neither chunk of *garden* means anything by itself), and a word can be broken into syllables for reading without any of those syllables being a meaningful word part. When a word is long enough to need syllable-by-syllable analysis, meaningful chunks like prefixes are one strategy among several — see [Multisyllabic Words](/skills/multisyllabic-words) for the fuller approach to breaking down longer words.

## How a prefix differs from a root

The base word a prefix attaches to here is always a whole, familiar English word that can stand on its own — *kind*, *happy*, *lock*, *read*, *tell*. That's different from a root, which is often not a complete word by itself. As students move into upper-elementary vocabulary, they meet prefixes attached to roots instead of whole base words — see [Greek and Latin Roots](/skills/greek-and-latin-roots) for that pattern, and [Common Prefixes](/skills/common-prefixes) for prefixes that begin to show it.

## When recognizing the prefix helps spelling

Spelling *unhappy* letter by letter is harder than spelling two known chunks: the *un-* prefix, and the base word *happy*, which a child likely already spells correctly on its own. The same is true for *reread* — a child who already knows *read* only has to add *re-* and remember not to drop the repeated *r*. Recognizing the prefix turns a six- or seven-letter word into two much shorter, more manageable pieces.

## Common mistakes

**Treating the prefix as a separate word.** *Unhappy* is one word, not *un* and *happy* written apart. Read and write it as a single unit.

**Dropping a letter at the boundary.** *Reread* and *rewrite* both keep every letter from both the prefix and the base word, even though that means writing the same letter twice in a row.

**Assuming every word that starts with the letters *re* or *un* has the prefix.** Words like *rest* and *real* only look like they start with *re-*. Removing "re" from *rest* doesn't leave a recognizable base word behind — "st" isn't a word — so those letters are just the start of the word, not the prefix. The same check works for *un-*: words like *uncle* and *under* aren't *un-* plus a base word either.

## A short teaching routine

1. Say the whole word, then say the prefix and the base word separately: *un- / happy*.
2. Ask what the base word means on its own.
3. Ask what the prefix adds or changes.
4. Put the two together and check the meaning against the whole word.
5. Spell the word in two chunks, prefix first, then the base word.

## If a learner struggles

Cover the prefix and ask the learner to spell the base word alone first — most of these base words are already familiar. Once the base is solid, uncover the prefix and add it as a single extra chunk at the front. If the base word alone is the hard part, the difficulty isn't really about prefixes yet; go back to spelling that word on its own before reintroducing the prefix.

## Signs the skill is becoming secure

Signs of security include separating a new prefix word into its prefix and base word without help, explaining what *un-* or *re-* contributes to a specific word, correctly identifying when a word merely starts with the letters *re* or *un* without actually containing the prefix, and spelling a familiar base word first before adding the prefix chunk.
