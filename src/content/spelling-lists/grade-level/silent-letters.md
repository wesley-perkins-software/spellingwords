### What Is a Silent Letter?

A silent letter is a letter that is written as part of a word's correct spelling but contributes no sound when the word is spoken. Say *wrap* out loud slowly: the word begins with the /r/ sound — there's no /w/ sound anywhere in it — yet *wrap* is spelled with a *w* before the *r*. That *w* is silent. The same thing happens with the *k* in *know* (said "noh," with no /k/ sound) and the *b* at the end of *thumb* (said "thum," with no /b/ sound).

This matters because it separates two things that feel like the same skill but aren't: sounding out a word, and spelling a word. A child who sounds out *wrap* carefully and accurately will hear /r/-/a/-/p/ — and still spell it wrong if they write only the sounds they hear. Silent letters are the clearest case where spelling by ear alone isn't enough; the letter has to be remembered as part of the word, not decoded from the sound.

This page covers three specific silent-letter patterns that show up often in early reading and writing: *wr* at the start of a word, *kn* at the start of a word, and *mb* at the end of a word. Many other silent-letter patterns exist in English — some are named in the FAQ below — but grouping too many different patterns together makes each one harder to learn securely. Three clear, bounded patterns are easier to master than a long, mixed list. Unlike some spelling conventions, these three don't come with a well-known list of exception words to watch for — once the pattern is learned, it applies reliably to new words that fit it.

### WR Words: A Silent W Before R

At the start of a word, when the letters *w* and *r* appear together, the *w* is silent and the word begins with the /r/ sound alone.

| Word | How it's said | Silent letter |
|---|---|---|
| wrap | rap | w |
| wrist | rist (rhymes with *fist*) | w |
| write | rite | w |

Dropping the silent *w* is the most common mistake, and with some *wr* words it does more than misspell the word — it spells a different real word instead. Leave the *w* off *wrap* and the result is *rap*, which is its own word (as in *rap* music, or a *rap* on the door). Leave it off *write* and the result, *rite*, is also a real word (a ceremonial *rite*), and sounds identical to another real word, *right*. This is worth naming directly: with *wr* words, a missing silent letter is sometimes not just "wrong" — it's a different, correctly spelled word that doesn't mean what the writer intended. *Wrist* doesn't have this effect — "rist" isn't a word — so the risk depends on the specific word, not on the *wr* pattern itself.

*Write* has a second silent letter working at the same time: the final *e*, which is part of a completely different pattern (see "How is this different from silent e?" below). Both silent letters belong to *write*'s correct spelling, but they aren't the same kind of silent letter, and a child doesn't need to understand the second one to learn the first.

### KN Words: A Silent K Before N

At the start of a word, when the letters *k* and *n* appear together, the *k* is silent and the word begins with the /n/ sound alone.

| Word | How it's said | Silent letter |
|---|---|---|
| know | no | k |
| knee | nee | k |
| knot | not | k |

Just like *wr*, dropping the silent *k* sometimes produces a different real word: *know* without its *k* is *no*, and *knot* without its *k* is *not*. Both pairs sound exactly alike, which makes them a useful thing to point out directly — a child who spells *no* when they mean *know* hasn't just missed a letter, they've written a real, different word.

### MB Words: A Silent B After M

At the end of a one-syllable word, when the letters *m* and *b* appear together, the *b* is silent and the word ends with the /m/ sound alone.

| Word | How it's said | Silent letter |
|---|---|---|
| thumb | thum | b |
| lamb | lam | b |
| crumb | crum | b |

This pattern works a little differently from *wr* and *kn*. Dropping the silent *b* usually produces a spelling that isn't a word at all — *thum* and *crum* aren't words — rather than a different real word. *Lamb* is a partial exception: *lam* is a real, if uncommon, word (an informal term for fleeing, as in "on the lam"), but it's obscure enough that a child mixing up the two words is unlikely. The mistake is still worth catching, but the "you accidentally spelled a different, meaningful word" risk that makes *wr* and *kn* errors so noticeable mostly doesn't apply here.

### What Should the Learner Notice?

- The silent letter is never optional — it's part of the word's correct spelling even though it isn't part of how the word sounds.
- In the words on this page, *wr* and *kn* appear at the start of the word, and *mb* appears at the end.
- Saying the word slowly doesn't reveal the silent letter — it can only be learned by seeing and remembering the word's spelling.
- With *wr* and *kn* words especially, leaving out the silent letter can spell a different real word, not just an incorrect one.

### Common Silent-Letter Mistakes

The mistake children make almost always in the same direction: leaving the silent letter out entirely, because it isn't heard. This is a reasonable strategy — it's exactly how skilled sound-based spelling works everywhere else — and it's precisely why *wr*, *kn*, and *mb* need to be taught as their own small, memorized patterns rather than sounded out.

A second, less common mistake is getting the silent letter's position wrong rather than leaving it out — writing *rwite* for *write*, or *nkow* for *know*. This usually means the pattern (silent letter, then the sounded consonant) hasn't been fully learned yet, even though the child correctly remembered that an extra letter belongs somewhere in the word.

A handful of *wr*, *kn*, and *mb* words are common enough — *write*, *know*, and *thumb* especially — that many children learn to write them correctly from memory well before they can explain why. That's a normal and useful stepping stone, not a sign the pattern needs to be taught differently.

### How to Practice Silent-Letter Words

1. Say the word slowly and clearly.
2. Ask: does this word start with *wr* or *kn*, or end with *mb*? Name the pattern before writing.
3. Write the word, including the silent letter, from memory.
4. Read back exactly what was written, letter by letter.
5. Check: is the silent letter there, and is it in the right place — before the sounded consonant for *wr*/*kn*, after the *m* for *mb*?

If a word comes out wrong, step 4 is the key one: read back exactly what was written and ask whether it spells the intended word, a different real word, or no word at all. For *wr* and *kn* words, that question often does the work by itself — a child who reads back "rap" when they meant "wrap" will usually notice the mismatch once it's said aloud, without being told which letter is missing.

### Signs Silent-Letter Words Are Becoming Secure

- Spelling a familiar *wr*, *kn*, or *mb* word correctly without sounding it out first.
- Noticing, without prompting, that leaving out the silent letter in a word like *wrap* or *knot* spells a different real word.
- Recognizing *wr* and *kn* at the start of an unfamiliar word and remembering that the first letter is silent.
- Explaining, in their own words, that some letters are part of a word's spelling even though they aren't part of how it sounds.

### How Is This Different From Silent E?

*Write* highlights a real distinction worth being clear about: this page is not about the silent *e* at the end of words like *make* or *hope*. That's a separate, unrelated pattern with its own page for each vowel (Long A Silent E Words, Long I Silent E Words, and so on) — there, the silent *e* isn't a meaningless leftover; it's doing a job, marking that the vowel before it says its own name. The *w* in *write* and the *k* in *know* don't mark anything — they simply aren't pronounced. A word can have both kinds of silent letter at once, as *write* does, without the two patterns having anything else in common.
