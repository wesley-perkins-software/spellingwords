A syllable is a spoken part of a word, usually built around a vowel sound. Count the parts you hear in the whole word rather than counting vowel letters. In _table_, the final _l_ can hold the second syllable even though the _e_ is silent. Syllables are one part of spelling a longer word: speech does not always reveal every letter, so familiar spelling patterns and meaningful word parts matter too.

## Start With the Spoken Word

Say the word naturally, then say it slowly without stretching it into extra syllables. Tap or mark each spoken part: _rab-bit_, _bas-ket_, _win-ter_. Next, look inside each part for a pattern you already know.

| Word     | Useful analysis | What to notice                                                                      |
| -------- | --------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| _rabbit_ | _rab_ + _bit_   | Both syllables have short vowels; the doubled _b_ helps keep the first vowel short. |
| _basket_ | _bas_ + _ket_   | Both syllables are closed by consonants and use short vowel sounds.                 |
| _winter_ | _win_ + _ter_   | The first syllable is closed; the ending has the common unstressed _-er_ spelling.  |

Open and closed syllable labels are useful clues, not guarantees for every English word. A **closed syllable** ends in one or more consonants and commonly has a short vowel, as in _rab_ and _ket_. An **open syllable** ends in a vowel; in many basic words, its vowel has a long sound. Stress, word history, and other spelling patterns can produce different results.

## Open Syllables

In _robot_, the first syllable is open: _ro-bot_. The _o_ ends _ro_ and has its long sound. In _paper_, _pa-_ is open and its _a_ is long. In _music_, _mu-_ is open; speakers may begin it with the long-u sound in _use_ or with the /oo/ sound in _moon_. That difference does not change the spelling.

When one consonant stands between two written vowels, trying the consonant with the following syllable can reveal an open first syllable: _ro-bot_, _pa-per_, _mu-sic_. This is a useful first attempt, not an absolute division rule. Say the whole word after dividing it and adjust if the result does not match a familiar pronunciation.

## Words Ending in Consonant-LE

At the end of words such as _table_ and _little_, a consonant plus _le_ forms the final syllable: _ta-ble_, _lit-tle_. The final _e_ is silent, and the ending usually sounds like “uhl”; the _l_ holds the syllable.

Work backward from the ending: circle consonant-_le_, say the first part, and join the parts again. Compare _ta + ble = table_ and _lit + tle = little_. The consonant immediately before _le_ belongs with the final syllable in this spelling convention. Words such as _while_ and _sale_ are outside this pattern because their endings do not form a separate syllable.

## Syllables and Meaning Parts Can Overlap

Some longer words become easier when meaning supplies the clearest chunks. _Sunset_ is both _sun-set_ and the compound _sun + set = sunset_. A word with a suffix may also preserve a known base spelling even when its spoken syllables are less obvious. Do not force every word into this kind of equation: _rabbit_ is not built from two smaller words _rab_ and _bit_. Use meaningful parts only when they are real.

## A Short Teaching Routine

1. **Say it normally.** Confirm the learner knows the word and its pronunciation.
2. **Mark the syllables.** Tap once for each spoken part, using the whole pronunciation rather than the number of vowel letters.
3. **Find useful chunks.** Look for a familiar syllable pattern or a genuine base, prefix, suffix, or compound part.
4. **Spell one chunk at a time.** Keep the whole word visible in memory while writing the parts in order.
5. **Check the whole word.** Read it aloud and inspect any unstressed vowel, doubled consonant, or known word part that hearing alone could not settle.

If a learner adds or loses a syllable, return to the natural spoken word before showing the spelling. If the number of syllables is right but a vowel is wrong, compare the syllable with a known pattern or word family. If an unstressed vowel is unclear, do not demand that the learner “sound it out harder”; supply the spelling, connect it to a known part or related word when possible, and revisit it through spaced practice.

The skill is becoming secure when the learner can preserve all the syllables in order, choose familiar patterns without treating them as exceptionless rules, use real meaning parts when they help, and identify the specific chunk that still needs checking.
