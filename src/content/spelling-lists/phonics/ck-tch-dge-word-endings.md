Four separate spelling conventions share the same underlying question: in a one-syllable word, what do you write when a sound lands right at the end, immediately after a single short vowel? *Ck* answers it for the /k/ sound. Doubling the final letter answers it for /f/, /l/, /s/, and sometimes /z/ — the pattern often called the FLOSS rule. *Tch* answers it for /ch/, and *dge* answers it for /j/. They are grouped on one page because they share that one question, not because they are one rule — each has its own condition, its own boundary, and its own exceptions, covered separately below.

### Final CK: Spelling /k/ at the End of a One-Syllable Word

The full condition has three parts: the word is one syllable, the /k/ sound falls at the very end of the word, and it is heard immediately after a single short vowel. When all three are true, spell the ending *ck*.

| Condition | Spelling | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| /k/ at the end of a one-syllable word, right after a single short vowel | ck | back, duck, rock, pick |
| /k/ at the end of a one-syllable word, right after another consonant | k | milk, pink, desk, bank |
| /k/ at the end of a one-syllable word, right after a long vowel or vowel team | k | bike, leak, book, cake |

Notice that the deciding question is never "does this word have a k sound at the end?" — almost every word here does. The deciding question is what comes immediately before it. A common overgeneralization is writing *ck* after a consonant (*milck* for *milk*) or after a long vowel (*bick* for a word that should end in *ke* or *k*); both slips come from skipping the "right after a short vowel" check.

A couple of familiar words break the pattern outright: *trek* and *yak* both meet the full condition — one syllable, short vowel, /k/ at the end — but are spelled with *k* alone. Both are borrowed words that never picked up the usual English spelling, and they're worth teaching by sight rather than treating as a reason to doubt the pattern.

### Doubled Final Consonants: the FLOSS Pattern

The same three-part condition applies here: one syllable, one short vowel, and the sound at the very end is /f/, /l/, or /s/ (sometimes /z/). When all three are true, double the final letter.

| Condition | Spelling | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| /f/, /l/, or /s/ at the end of a one-syllable word, right after a single short vowel | double the letter | staff, bell, moss, cliff |
| /z/ at the end of a one-syllable word, right after a single short vowel | sometimes doubled | buzz, fuzz, jazz |
| Any of these sounds after a consonant blend, not directly after the vowel | single letter | shelf, golf, else |

Doubling only applies when a single short vowel sits directly against the final sound. In *shelf* or *else*, two consonant sounds follow the vowel, not one, so the doubling question doesn't come up at all — this is a common overgeneralization to watch for (writing *shelff* for *shelf*). Of the four letters, *z* is the least reliable: many high-frequency words with a genuine final /z/ sound spelled *z* stay single (*quiz*), so it's worth teaching *z* cautiously rather than as confidently as *f*, *l*, and *s*.

The exceptions to *f* and *s* doubling aren't all the same kind of exception, and it helps to keep them apart:

- **Irregular single-f spellings.** *If*, *chef*, and *clef* meet the full condition — one syllable, short vowel, final /f/ — but simply don't double. (*Chef* and *clef* are French loanwords that kept their original spelling.)
- **The word *of* is not really an exception at all.** Its final letter *f* actually stands for the /v/ sound, not /f/ — say it aloud and compare it with *if*. Since the sound doesn't match the condition in the first place, doubling was never on the table.
- **Irregular single-s spellings.** *Yes*, *gas*, *bus*, *plus*, and *this* meet the full condition — one syllable, short vowel, final /s/ — but don't double.
- **Words where final s means /z/, not /s/.** *Is*, *was*, *his*, *as*, and *has* end in the /z/ sound even though they're spelled with *s*. Because the FLOSS condition is specifically about the /s/ sound, these words never met it — they aren't rule-breakers, they were just never candidates.

### TCH and CH: Two Spellings for the Same /ch/ Sound

The same shape again: one syllable, /ch/ at the very end of the word, immediately after a single short vowel. When all three are true, spell it *tch*.

A word simply containing the letters "c-h" is not automatically an example of the plain CH digraph — check what comes right before those letters. If it's a single short vowel with nothing in between, the true ending is *tch*, not *ch*, even though the letters "c-h" are technically part of it.

| Condition | Spelling | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| /ch/ at the end of a one-syllable word, right after a single short vowel | tch | catch, pitch, notch, hutch |
| /ch/ at the end of a one-syllable word, right after a consonant | ch | bench, lunch, ranch |
| /ch/ at the end of a one-syllable word, right after a long vowel or vowel team | ch | reach, coach, pooch |

Four high-frequency words break the pattern: *much*, *such*, *which*, and *rich* all follow a short vowel but are spelled with plain *ch*. They're frequent enough in early reading that they're usually taught by sight, alongside the regular *tch* pattern rather than instead of it.

### DGE and GE: Two Spellings for the Same /j/ Sound

The condition matches the other three: one syllable, /j/ at the very end of the word, immediately after a single short vowel. When all three are true, spell it *dge*.

| Condition | Spelling | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| /j/ at the end of a one-syllable word, right after a single short vowel | dge | badge, bridge, lodge, fudge |
| /j/ at the end of a one-syllable word, right after a consonant such as n or l | ge | change, bulge, hinge |
| /j/ at the end of a one-syllable word, right after a long vowel | ge | page, cage, huge |

A word like *change* can look like a short-vowel exception at first, since it doesn't sound like *page* or *cage*. It isn't one: the /j/ sound in *change* is heard right after the consonant *n*, not right after the vowel, so *ge* is the expected spelling once you check what's actually next to the /j/ sound rather than only which vowel the word contains. *Strange* and *range* work the same way. Treating these as a "rule-breaker" is a common overgeneralization worth naming directly.

This page's scope is one-syllable words, and that boundary matters here more than for the other three conventions: everyday words like *cabbage*, *village*, *message*, and *damage* end in an unstressed */ij/* sound spelled *-age*, not *-adge*. They aren't exceptions to the convention above — they're outside it, because they're two syllables with the /j/ sound in an unstressed final syllable rather than a stressed one-syllable ending. Naming this keeps a learner from overgeneralizing *dge* onto longer, unstressed endings once they've learned it confidently in short words.

### A Routine for Choosing the Right Ending

1. Say the whole word aloud.
2. Identify the final sound: is it /k/, /f/, /l/, /s/, /z/, /ch/, or /j/?
3. Identify the vowel sound immediately before that final sound.
4. Check the conditions: is the word one syllable, and does the vowel come directly against the final sound with nothing else — no other consonant — in between?
5. If both conditions hold, choose the matching convention (ck, doubled letter, tch, or dge). If either condition doesn't hold, use the plain spelling (k, single letter, ch, or ge) instead.
6. Write the word, then read it back.

If a word comes out wrong, the fastest way to help is to read back exactly what was written and ask which condition doesn't match — was the vowel sound really short, was anything really in between it and the final sound, or is this simply one of the familiar exceptions? Naming *which* condition was missed, rather than supplying the correct spelling directly, is what lets the decision transfer to the next new word.

### Signs These Conventions Are Becoming Secure

- Identifying which of the four conventions a familiar word demonstrates.
- Explaining, in their own words, why a word uses one ending instead of its plain counterpart (ck instead of k, tch instead of ch, dge instead of ge).
- Spelling a new, regular one-syllable word correctly using the matching convention.
- Recognizing a familiar exception, like *which* or *trek*, without concluding the general convention is wrong.
- Telling *ch* apart from *tch*, and *ge* apart from *dge*, in an unfamiliar word.
