### Long I Word Families

| Family | Words |
|---|---|
| -ike | bike, hike, like, spike |
| -ine | fine, line, mine, nine, pine, shine |
| -ide | hide, ride, side, slide, wide |
| -ime | chime, dime, lime, time |

### What Is the Long I Silent E Pattern?

Long i is the vowel sound that says its own name — "eye." In words like *bike* and *time*, a final silent e signals that the i earlier in the word should say that long sound instead of the short i heard in *sit*. It's the same silent-e, or vowel-consonant-e (VCe), pattern already met in Long A Silent E Words, applied to a different vowel — proof that the pattern is general, not tied to one letter.

### How the Pattern Transfers From Long A

A learner who can explain why *cake* isn't spelled *cak* already has what's needed to explain why *bike* isn't spelled *bik*: say the word, notice the long vowel, add the silent e at the end. The mechanics don't change from Long A Silent E Words — only the vowel does.

### What Should the Learner Notice?

- The silent e still makes no sound of its own; it still changes the vowel two letters back.
- The pattern still works with more than one consonant in the middle, as in *smile* and *slide*.
- Right after learning this rule, a learner may start adding a silent e to short-vowel words that don't need one — that's a normal, temporary overgeneralization, not a step backward.

### Long I Compared With Short I

| Short i | Long i |
|---|---|
| pin | pine |
| kit | kite |
| rid | ride |
| hid | hide |

### Common Long I Mistakes

The overgeneralization mentioned above is the most common mistake at this stage: a learner who has just learned "silent e makes the vowel long" may start adding a silent e to already-correct short-vowel words, spelling *pig* as *pige* or *sit* as *site*. This isn't a sign of confusion — it's a sign the rule has been learned as a rule, just not yet bounded. Practicing pairs like *pin*/*pine* side by side, so the learner has to choose rather than default to adding an e, usually resolves it.

A second exception worth naming: *give* and *live* (the verb, as in *I live here*) keep a silent e but stay short, as in *sit* — the same "a common word almost never ends in v" spelling convention behind *have* on the Long A page, not a vowel-lengthening e. *Live* is also spelled the same whether it means "I live here" (short i) or "a live performance" (long i); which one applies is decided by context, not spelling — a detail worth mentioning only if it comes up.

### How to Practice Long I Silent E Words

1. Say the target word aloud.
2. Stretch it into its sounds together, noticing that the final e adds no sound of its own (*b... ī... k*).
3. Write or type the letters in order, including the silent e.
4. Read the result back exactly as written.

If a word comes out wrong, step 4 is the key one: read back exactly what was written — "that says *bik*" — and let the learner listen for what's missing, rather than pointing to the correct letters directly. Hearing the mismatch themselves is what builds the skill; being handed the answer skips it.

### Signs the Long I Silent E Pattern Is Becoming Secure

Signs the pattern is becoming secure include:

- Hearing long i in a new, unfamiliar word.
- Telling a long-i silent-e word apart from a short-i word that looks similar, like *pin* and *pine*.
- Reading a long-i silent-e word not seen before.
- Spelling a representative word from dictation.
- Catching and correcting their own over-applied silent e on a word that doesn't need one.
