*C* and *g* each have a hard sound and a soft sound. Hard *c* says /k/, as in *cat*. Hard *g* says its own sound, /g/, as in *game*. But both letters change when the very next letter is *e*, *i*, or *y*: *c* softens to /s/, the sound at the start of *city* and *ice*. *G* softens to /j/, the sound at the start of *giant* and *page*. This is a genuine spelling pattern, reliable enough to use when deciding how to spell a word — not just a rule for reading.

### How the Condition Works

Both letters change for the same reason, so the condition is worth stating once for both:

| The next letter is... | *c* usually says | *g* usually says |
|---|---|---|
| e, i, or y | /s/ — soft (city, ice, icy) | /j/ — soft (giant, page, gym) |
| a, o, u, or a consonant | /k/ — hard (cat, cot, cup) | /g/ — hard (game, got, gum) |

Soft *c* and soft *g* almost always show up right before *e*, *i*, or *y* — that direction is dependable enough to trust. The reverse isn't guaranteed, though: seeing *e*, *i*, or *y* after a *c* or *g* doesn't always mean it will be soft. That gap matters much more for *g* than for *c*, which is why each letter gets its own look below.

### Soft C: A Reliable Pattern

Soft c shows up in all three trigger positions: *city* (before *i*), *ice* (before *e*), *icy* (before *y*). Real exceptions among common words are rare — a child who applies "soft c before e, i, or y" can trust it in nearly every word they'll meet at this level.

### Soft G: A Pattern With Real Exceptions

Soft g also shows up in all three positions: *giant* (before *i*), *page* (before *e*), *gym* (before *y*). But soft g is noticeably less dependable than soft c. Some of the most common words in a child's vocabulary keep the hard /g/ sound even though g is followed by e or i:

- *get* — hard g, not "jet"
- *give* — hard g, not "jive"
- *girl* — hard g, not "jirl"
- *gift* — hard g, not a soft-g word

This is the single most important thing to watch for with this pattern. A child who has just learned "g goes soft before e, i, or y" will often overapply it to these very familiar words — reading *get* as if it started like *gem*, or second-guessing how to spell it. Because *get*, *give*, *girl*, and *gift* are so common, it's worth naming this directly rather than letting a child discover it through repeated correction: soft g is a strong pattern, not an absolute one, and these four words are worth learning by sight as the exceptions to watch for.

### What Should the Learner Notice?

- The rule is about the letter written right after c or g — not about how the whole word sounds or feels.
- A silent letter still counts. In *ice* and *page*, the final e is silent — written but not pronounced — yet it's still the letter that makes the c or g in front of it soft. Long I Silent E Words and Long A Silent E Words cover why words like *ice* and *page* end in a silent e in the first place.
- One word can contain both the hard and soft version of the same letter. In *cage*, the first c is hard (before a) and the g is soft (before e). In *circus*, the first c is soft (before i) and the second c is hard (before u). Reading letter-by-letter, rather than guessing from the whole word, is what makes this pattern usable.

### Why Bridge Isn't Spelled "Brige"

Soft g explains a spelling choice from another Skill. Words like *bridge*, *badge*, and *edge* need the /j/ sound right after a short vowel — but they can't simply add e after the g (as in *page*), because adding a silent e after a single consonant is exactly the signal that turns a short vowel into a long one (the pattern behind *page*, *cake*, and the rest of the Silent E family). Spelling it "brige" would suggest a long i, not the short i in *bridge*. English uses *dge* instead, which keeps the /j/ sound without disturbing the vowel before it. The CK, Double Letters, and TCH/DGE Word Endings Skill covers this ending in full; soft g is what makes the workaround necessary in the first place.

### A Routine for Practicing Soft C and Soft G

1. Say the word aloud and listen for /s/, /j/, /k/, or /g/ at the target spot.
2. Look at (or predict) the letter right after c or g.
3. Match the sound to the letter: e, i, or y usually means soft; a, o, u, or a consonant usually means hard.
4. Write the word, checking that the letter chosen for the sound is actually c or g rather than s, k, or j.
5. For get, give, girl, and gift, skip the letter check and rely on the word itself being familiar by sight.

When a child spells a soft-sound word with the wrong letter — writing *jem* for *gem*, or *sirkus* for *circus* — resist supplying the correct spelling right away. Ask what letter usually comes after a soft c or soft g, and let the child check the word against that condition themselves, rather than simply being told the fix.

### Signs This Pattern Is Becoming Secure

Signs the pattern is becoming secure include: predicting whether an unfamiliar c or g will be hard or soft by checking the next letter; reading and spelling *get*, *give*, *girl*, and *gift* correctly without hesitating over the g; noticing both sounds inside a single word like *cage* or *circus*; and explaining, in their own words, why *bridge* uses *dge* instead of a plain g.
