### AU and AW Grouped by Position

| Spelling | Where it typically appears | Words |
|---|---|---|
| aw | End of a word, or before n or l | saw, draw, lawn, crawl, hawk |
| au | Middle of a word | cause, author |

*Haul* is deliberately left out of this table — see below.

Reading each row aloud shows the same vowel sound in every word — a sound that's neither short nor long — with only the spelling and its typical position changing.

### What Are the AU and AW Spellings?

AU and AW are two-letter spellings for one vowel sound: the sound heard in *saw* and *cause*, distinct from any short or long vowel sound taught earlier. Neither letter is silent and neither is sounded separately — together the two letters spell a single vowel sound, the same way other vowel teams do.

### Why AW Tends to Appear at the End (or Before N/L) and AU in the Middle

In the common words taught here, *aw* is typically found at the very end of a word, as in *saw* and *draw*, or immediately before *n* or *l*, as in *lawn*, *crawl*, and *hawk*'s close relatives *dawn* and *shawl*. *Au* is typically found in the middle of a word followed by a consonant other than *n* or *l*, as in *cause* and *author*. This is a genuinely useful tendency for choosing a first guess — but it's a tendency, not an absolute rule, and it's worth naming the words that break it rather than pretending they don't exist.

### A Genuine Exception Worth Knowing

*Haul* uses *au* directly before *l* — exactly the position where the tendency predicts *aw*. *Fault* and *vault* do the same thing. These aren't rare or obscure words; they're common enough that a learner will meet them, so the honest version of the tendency is "aw is common before n or l, but not guaranteed," not "aw always comes before n or l."

That's an exception of *position* — *au* shows up where *aw* was expected, but the sound is still the target sound. A different, more fundamental exception exists in *laugh*: there, *au* doesn't spell the target sound at all. *Laugh* rhymes with *half*, not with *haul*, so it's not a case of predicting the wrong spelling for the right sound — it's a completely different sound wearing this page's letters. Because *laugh* is such a common, early word, it's worth naming directly rather than letting a learner discover the mismatch on their own.

### What Should the Learner Notice?

- AU and AW spell the exact same vowel sound — the difference is spelling only, not sound.
- *Aw* is the better first guess at the end of a word or right before *n* or *l*; *au* is the better first guess elsewhere in the middle of a word.
- *Haul* is a genuine exception to the *n*/*l* tendency and needs to be learned as its own word rather than worked out from the pattern.
- *Laugh* is a different kind of exception: the letters *au* are there, but the target sound isn't — it needs to be learned by sight, not sounded out.
- *Augh*, as in *caught* and *taught*, is a related but separate spelling, and isn't even consistent with itself (compare *caught* with *laugh*) — a further reason it's left for its own lesson rather than folded into this page.

### How to Practice AU and AW Words

1. Say the target word aloud and confirm it has the *saw*/*cause* vowel sound.
2. Listen for whether the sound is at the very end of the word, right before *n* or *l*, or somewhere else in the middle.
3. Choose *aw* for the end or before *n*/*l*, or *au* elsewhere — then check the word against ones already known, since a small number of words (like *haul*) don't follow the tendency.
4. Write or type the letters in order, then read the result back exactly as written.

If a learner spells *haul* as *hawl*, that's the tendency working exactly as expected — not a mistake in reasoning. Confirm the guess was reasonable, then treat *haul* as one to learn by sight alongside the regular pattern, the same way any genuine exception gets learned.

### Signs the AU/AW Pattern Is Becoming Secure

Signs the pattern is becoming secure include:

- Reading a new *au* or *aw* word aloud with the correct vowel sound.
- Choosing *au* or *aw* correctly for an unfamiliar regular word based on its position.
- Spelling a representative word from dictation, including recognizing *haul* as a word to learn by sight rather than by the tendency.
- Explaining, in their own words, why *author* uses *au* even though it isn't right next to *n* or *l*.
