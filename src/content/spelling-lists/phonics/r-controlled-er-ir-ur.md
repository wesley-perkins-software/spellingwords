### Three Spellings for One Sound

Say *her*, *bird*, and *turn* aloud, one after another. The vowel sound in the middle is the same sound each time, even though it's spelled three different ways: *er*, *ir*, and *ur*. This is one of the trickier discoveries in English spelling — three letter combinations that all do the same job.

- **ER words:** her, fern, term
- **IR words:** bird, first, girl
- **UR words:** turn, hurt, turtle

Reading each group aloud confirms that none of the three spellings sounds more "correct" than the others — they're equally valid ways of writing the same sound. This is a representative set, not a complete one.

### Why There's No Rule to Predict the Spelling

Unlike AR and OR, where one spelling reliably matches one sound, ER, IR, and UR ask a learner to remember which of three spellings belongs to each specific word. There's no dependable position rule, no neighboring-letter rule, and no shortcut that works across high-frequency words — *bird* isn't spelled *ir* for a reason a first grader can apply to a new word, it simply is. The practical strategy is exposure: reading and writing each word enough times that the correct spelling starts to look right, the same way many high-frequency words are learned.

### The Sound in Short Words Versus Longer Words

The r-controlled sound is clearest in short, stressed, one-syllable words like *her*, *bird*, *turn*, *first*, *girl*, *hurt*, and *turtle* — say any of them alone, and the vowel sound stands out clearly. In longer words like *sister*, *winter*, and *under*, the same spelling (*er*, in all three of these) appears at the end, but the syllable isn't stressed, so the sound comes out softer and less distinct — closer to a quick, relaxed "er" than the fuller sound in *her*. Both are the same underlying r-controlled sound; the difference is just how clearly a syllable is stressed, not a different spelling pattern.

**A note on accent:** this page describes the sound as heard in mainstream American English, where r is always pronounced. In some accents — including many British and a few American ones — r isn't pronounced after a vowel the same way, which changes how these words sound without changing how they're spelled.

### What Should the Learner Notice

- ER, IR, and UR sound identical in words like *her*, *bird*, and *turn* — the ear can't tell them apart, only memory of the specific word can.
- The same sound also appears, softer and less stressed, at the end of longer words like *sister* and *winter*.
- A small group of words spelled with *wor-* — *word*, *work*, *world* — make this same sound too, even though they're spelled with o instead of e, i, or u (see R-Controlled OR Words).
- Earlier, *her* and words like *term* were introduced as a pattern separate from short e (see Short E Words) — this page is where that pattern is taught in full.

### How to Help a Learner Practice ER, IR, and UR Words

1. Say the target word aloud and notice the r-controlled sound.
2. Look at the word in print and notice which of the three spellings it uses.
3. Write the word, then read it back.
4. Repeat with the same word across several short sessions, since remembering the spelling — not sounding it out — is the actual skill being built.

Because there's no rule to fall back on, the diagnostic response here is different from most other spelling patterns: if a learner writes the wrong one of the three spellings, don't ask them to sound it out again — the sound is identical no matter which spelling they choose. Instead, show them the correct spelling, say the word together, and plan to revisit that specific word again soon. Repetition across several encounters, not a single correction, is what builds accurate spelling here.

### Signs the Pattern Is Becoming Secure

Signs the pattern is becoming secure include:

- Hearing the r-controlled sound in *her*, *bird*, or *turn* and recognizing it as one sound, however it's spelled.
- Correctly spelling familiar ER, IR, and UR words from dictation.
- Noticing the same sound in an unstressed ending, like *sister* or *winter*.
- Reading a new ER, IR, or UR word without hesitating over which sound it makes, even without knowing the spelling in advance.
