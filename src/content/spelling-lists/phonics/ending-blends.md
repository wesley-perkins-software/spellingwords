### What Is an Ending Consonant Blend?

An ending consonant blend is two consonant sounds sitting side by side at the end of a word, with each sound still heard. In *hand*, both the /n/ and the /d/ are heard after the vowel; in *jump*, both /m/ and /p/ are heard. Nothing merges and nothing goes silent — a learner who listens all the way to the end of the word should be able to hear two distinct final sounds, not one.

### Why Ending Blends Are Often Harder to Hear

A beginning blend gets a crisp, deliberate start — it's the first thing spoken, and speakers tend to pronounce it clearly. An ending blend is different: it's the last thing spoken, often right before a pause or the next word, and the final sound in that pair is the one most likely to get clipped or trail off in fast, casual speech. Say *hand* quickly in a sentence — "hand me that" — and the final /d/ can almost disappear. This is exactly why ending blends deserve slower, more deliberate articulation during practice than beginning blends usually need.

### Common Ending Blend Families

| Blend group | Blends | Example words |
|---|---|---|
| Nasal blends (often clipped in fast speech) | mp, nd, nk, nt | jump, hand, sink, tent |
| Other ending blends | ft, lk, lt, sk | gift, milk, belt, desk |

Nasal blends — where the first sound is made partly through the nose (m, n) — are worth calling out separately, since they're the ones most prone to sounding like just one consonant in quick speech, even though two letters and two sounds still belong there.

### What Should the Learner Notice?

- Both final consonant sounds are heard, in order, right after the vowel.
- The second sound in the pair is the one most likely to fade in fast speech — it deserves extra attention, not less.
- The same word said slowly (*h... a... n... d*) reveals both final sounds clearly, even when the same word said quickly does not.

### Careful Articulation and Rereading

Because ending blends are easy to underhear, the most useful habit here isn't a new listening trick — it's slowing down on purpose and checking the written result afterward. Say the target word crisply, all the way through, without letting the last sound trail off. Then, after writing it, read it back exactly as written and compare it to the word actually intended. A written word that's missing its final consonant almost always reveals itself this way, even when it sounded fine while being said quickly the first time.

Ending blends are also where a beginning digraph's own final-position cousin briefly matters: a word like *much* or *such* ends in the single fused sound of the CH digraph, not in two separate consonant sounds the way *jump* or *hand* do — so a learner reading back a word that ends in a blend should expect to hear (and spell) two letters doing two jobs, not one letter pair doing one.

### Common Ending Blend Mistakes

The most common mistake is dropping the second, final consonant — writing *han* for *hand*, or *jum* for *jump*. This is a direct result of how easily that sound gets clipped in natural speech, not a sign the learner doesn't understand the pattern. Saying the word deliberately slowly, holding the final consonant a beat longer than feels natural, usually surfaces the missing letter right away.

### How to Practice Ending Blend Words

1. Say the target word aloud, all the way through, without letting the ending trail off.
2. Stretch it into its sounds together, holding onto both final consonants (*m... i... l... k*).
3. Write or type one letter for each sound, in order.
4. Read the result back exactly as written.

If a word comes out wrong, step 4 is the key one: read back exactly what was written — "that says *mil*" — and let the learner listen for which final sound is missing, rather than pointing to the correct letter directly. Hearing the gap themselves, at the end of the word where it's easiest to miss, is what builds the skill.

### Signs the Ending Blend Pattern Is Becoming Secure

Signs the pattern is becoming secure include:

- Hearing both final consonant sounds in a new, unfamiliar ending-blend word.
- Spelling a representative ending-blend word from dictation without dropping the last sound.
- Rereading their own writing and catching a dropped final consonant independently.
- Reading an ending-blend word not seen before.
- Explaining, in their own words, why the last sound in a word like *hand* or *jump* is easy to miss but still needs to be spelled.
