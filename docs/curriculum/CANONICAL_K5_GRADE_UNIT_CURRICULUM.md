## Framing the synthesis

This report translates the previously established K–5 spelling taxonomy into a canonical Grade Unit curriculum: a grade-by-grade educational blueprint that can anchor product decisions later, without being confused with pages, navigation, or implementation. I treated the earlier taxonomy as authoritative and used only targeted verification to confirm the major scope-and-sequence decisions against primary standards and major structured-literacy curricula. That limited verification was necessary because the field does not have a single universally accepted spelling scope and sequence; even LETRS states that grade levels are approximate and that “there is no one accepted scope and sequence in the field.”

The synthesis rests on a broad area of agreement across standards and curricula. In the early grades, instruction begins with letter-sound correspondences, short vowels, basic word encoding, and high-frequency words; then it expands into consonant digraphs and blends, long-vowel spellings, vowel teams, syllables, and inflectional endings; after that it shifts into broader vowel patterns, multisyllabic words, morphology, and conventional spelling; and by upper elementary it relies increasingly on roots, affixes, syllabication, word relationships, and meaning-based spelling. Common Core, New York, Florida, and Texas all show this general progression, even when they differ in exact placement or terminology.

The key design decision in this report is that not every concept becomes a Grade Unit. Some concepts are strong, teacher-recognizable instructional topics and should stand as units. Others are better treated as embedded knowledge, recurring strands, or internal dependencies. That distinction is educationally responsible because spelling is not just a sequence of weekly lists; it draws simultaneously on phonology, orthography, morphology, and meaning. The International Literacy Association explicitly cautions against reducing spelling to rote memorization and argues that students use multiple “threads” of spelling knowledge across grades; Louisa Moats likewise argues that continued spelling instruction should move beyond rules alone to roots, prefixes, suffixes, word families, and language of origin.

Using that framework, I applied four rules to decide what becomes a Grade Unit. First, the unit name must be recognizable to elementary teachers and understandable to parents. Second, the unit must be a coherent instructional topic, not a tiny subpattern. Third, the grade assigned should be the grade of primary introduction, not every year of later review. Fourth, concepts that are taught every year—especially high-frequency words, dictation, proofreading, and transfer into writing—should be treated as spiral strands, not duplicated as new units in multiple grades. That approach is also consistent with Texas TEKS, which says the strands are recursive and integrated, and with ILA’s recommendation that multiple types of spelling knowledge be taught across grades rather than in a rigid one-thread-at-a-time hierarchy.

## Kindergarten through Grade Two

### Kindergarten overview

Kindergarten spelling instruction is primarily about establishing the alphabetic principle for encoding: students connect sounds to letters, segment and represent simple phonemes, spell short-vowel CVC words, and begin to store high-frequency words that are too early or too irregular to be handled entirely through current phonics knowledge. Common Core and New York both place one-to-one consonant correspondences, short-vowel spellings, and common sight words in kindergarten; Fundations Level K similarly targets CVC spelling, short vowels, basic digraph exposure, and an initial bank of high-frequency “trick” words. UFLI’s early sequence also begins with alphabet knowledge, VC/CVC words, and very early short-vowel encoding.

| Grade Unit                          | Purpose                                                                                                 | Why it belongs in Kindergarten                                                                                                         | Prerequisites                                                           | Prepares students for                                                          |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Sounds, Letters, and Early Encoding | Establish one-to-one sound-letter mapping in spelling; connect phoneme segmentation to writing letters. | This is the entry point for spelling in every major standards set and foundational curriculum.                                         | Oral language, phonological awareness, letter exposure.                 | Short-vowel spelling, CVC words, early dictation, high-frequency-word mapping. |
| Short Vowels and CVC Words          | Teach students to spell simple closed one-syllable words with short vowels.                             | Kindergarten is the first stable point where children move from sounds in speech to phonetic spelling of full words.                   | Basic consonant sounds, phoneme segmentation, sound-symbol association. | Digraphs, blends, inflectional endings, silent e, syllable work.               |
| High-Frequency Words                | Introduce the most useful common words, including partially or fully irregular spellings.               | Standards and programs consistently expect sight/high-frequency-word work to begin immediately, but as a distinct strand from phonics. | Basic print awareness and early letter-sound knowledge.                 | Ongoing irregular-word study, sentence dictation, reading and writing fluency. |

The recommended ordering is: Sounds, Letters, and Early Encoding → Short Vowels and CVC Words → High-Frequency Words. In practice, the third unit begins early and runs across the year, but it should be framed after the first two because students need some alphabetic skill before high-frequency-word instruction becomes efficient. This ordering matches the convergence among Common Core, New York, Fundations, and UFLI.

The main kindergarten concepts that should not become separate Grade Units are phonological awareness, handwriting/letter formation, sentence dictation, and early digraph exposure. They are essential, but they function better as embedded supports inside the kindergarten units rather than as independent yearlong topics. That is especially true because standards and curricula treat these foundations as intertwined rather than separate silos.

> **Implementation note:** Kindergarten is now implemented on the public website. The three broad curriculum units above are presented through eight finer-grained Core Spelling cards, four High-Frequency Words sets containing 40 words, and three bounded Additional Practice cards. The focused short-vowel pages and late-kindergarten digraph preview make the learning path easier to navigate without changing the curriculum’s scope or progression.

### Grade One overview

In Grade 1, spelling instruction broadens from basic closed-syllable words into new one-syllable patterns and the first systematic longer-word work. Common Core Grade 1 explicitly includes common consonant digraphs, final-e and common vowel-team conventions, basic syllable counting, two-syllable decoding by breaking words into syllables, inflectional endings, and irregular words. New York and Florida show the same general pattern. Fundations Level 1 and CKLA Grade 1 likewise move from short-vowel one-syllable words into long-vowel spellings, vowel teams, syllable concepts, and continued tricky-word work.

| Grade Unit                       | Purpose                                                                                        | Why it belongs in Grade 1                                                                                                                       | Prerequisites                                           | Prepares students for                                                                 |
| -------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Consonant Digraphs and Blends    | Extend one-syllable spelling beyond plain CVC words by teaching common consonant combinations. | Most standards and curricula place digraphs firmly here, and blends are commonly consolidated here even when lightly previewed in kindergarten. | Short-vowel CVC spelling; consonant sound knowledge.    | More complex one-syllable words, inflectional endings, multisyllabic decoding.        |
| Inflectional Endings             | Teach -s, -es, -ed, and -ing as spelling patterns attached to readable base words.             | Grade 1 is where these endings become productive and frequent in both reading and spelling.                                                     | One-syllable words; phoneme segmentation; short vowels. | Prefixes, suffixes, suffix rules, plurals, word-family awareness.                     |
| Silent E and Long Vowels         | Introduce the first major long-vowel spelling system.                                          | Common Core, Florida, New York, and most phonics programs place final-e long vowels in Grade 1.                                                 | Short vowels; CVC contrast.                             | Vowel teams, open syllables, suffix rules such as dropping silent e.                  |
| Vowel Teams                      | Introduce the most common long-vowel teams in a controlled sequence.                           | Grade 1 is the standard point where students move past silent e into additional long-vowel spellings.                                           | Long-vowel concept; one-syllable decoding security.     | Diphthongs, alternative vowel spellings, r-controlled contrast, advanced vowel study. |
| Syllables and Two-Syllable Words | Teach students to hear, count, and break words into syllables for spelling and reading.        | Standards in Grade 1 consistently expect two-syllable work once students control basic one-syllable patterns.                                   | Short and long one-syllable words; vowel knowledge.     | Syllable types, multisyllabic words, affixes, advanced word analysis.                 |

The best Grade 1 ordering is: Consonant Digraphs and Blends → Inflectional Endings → Silent E and Long Vowels → Vowel Teams → Syllables and Two-Syllable Words. That order preserves the logic that students first stabilize one-syllable consonant patterns, then work with common endings on familiar words, then shift into long-vowel spellings, and finally apply those patterns in longer words. Commercial curricula vary somewhat—some place long-vowel work before much endings work—but the overall dependency chain remains stable.

The concepts absorbed into Grade 1 units rather than made separate units are open and closed syllables, schwa-free early syllable division, and continued high-frequency-word work. Open/closed syllables are real concepts, but at this stage they function better inside Syllables and Two-Syllable Words than as separate Grade Units. High-frequency words continue every year, but Grade 1 does not need a second standalone unit because the strand was already introduced in kindergarten.

> **Implementation note:** Grade 1 is now implemented on the public website. The five broad curriculum units above map to twelve more granular Core Spelling cards; closely related patterns are separated when a focused public practice step improves sequencing and navigation. The site also presents six cumulative Common Words sets containing 72 unique words and three bounded Additional Practice cards. The dedicated **Open Syllables and Final Y** card is a focused presentation of the syllable work described above, and the Grade 1 **R-Controlled Vowels** card is an intentional early instructional treatment within the timing range acknowledged later in this research. Neither changes the canonical progression: the public architecture makes its components more explicit.

### Grade Two overview

Grade 2 is where the curriculum typically consolidates the full basic phonics code and begins systematic multisyllabic spelling. Common Core moves students into additional common vowel teams, regularly spelled two-syllable words with long vowels, and words with common prefixes and suffixes. New York expects two-syllable decoding and recognition of root words with common prefixes and suffixes. Florida explicitly lists variable vowel teams and diphthongs, open and closed syllables, consonant-le, common prefixes and suffixes, and silent-letter combinations. Fundations Level 2 and HD Word likewise show Grade 2 work on six syllable types, r-controlled vowels, vowel teams, unexpected vowel patterns, consonant-le, hard/soft c and g, and increasingly complex multisyllabic words.

| Grade Unit                                  | Purpose                                                                                               | Why it belongs in Grade 2                                                                                                                         | Prerequisites                                                         | Prepares students for                                                                     |
| ------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| R-Controlled Vowels                         | Teach ar, or, er, ir, and ur as a distinct spelling family.                                           | These patterns typically appear after long-vowel systems are established and before advanced morphology.                                          | Long-vowel patterns; one- and two-syllable decoding.                  | Multisyllabic words, spelling options, advanced vowel analysis.                           |
| Diphthongs and Other Vowel Patterns         | Teach vowel patterns such as oi/oy, ou/ow, oo, au/aw, and less-regular vowel spellings.               | Grade 2 is the common point where instruction broadens from the most common long-vowel teams to other major vowel patterns.                       | Silent e, common vowel teams, short/long contrast.                    | Advanced vowel pattern work, homophone distinctions, multisyllabic spelling.              |
| Syllable Types and Multisyllabic Words      | Formalize syllable categories and use them to spell longer words.                                     | Standards and programs converge strongly on Grade 2 as the point where syllable-type knowledge becomes systematic.                                | Syllable counting, two-syllable decoding, vowel pattern knowledge.    | Advanced multisyllabic words, affixes, schwa, final stable syllables.                     |
| Silent Letters and Ending Spelling Patterns | Group common orthographic conventions such as silent-letter combinations and final spelling patterns. | Programs often teach these after students control the basic code, because the patterns are easier to learn as refinements of already-known words. | Basic one-syllable accuracy; digraph/blend knowledge; vowel patterns. | Hard/soft spellings, morphological spelling rules, conventional spelling.                 |
| Hard and Soft C and G                       | Teach the main positional pattern that changes c and g pronunciations and spellings.                  | This concept typically appears once students can handle alternative vowel patterns and are ready for more conditional spelling patterns.          | Basic c and g spellings; vowel pattern knowledge.                     | Greek/Latin roots, derived spellings such as magic/magician, advanced orthographic study. |

The recommended Grade 2 ordering is: R-Controlled Vowels → Diphthongs and Other Vowel Patterns → Syllable Types and Multisyllabic Words → Silent Letters and Ending Spelling Patterns → Hard and Soft C and G. The order moves from larger vowel families into the syllable framework that organizes longer words, then into increasingly conventional and conditional patterns. Some programs place hard/soft c and g slightly earlier or slightly later, but broad consensus still places it after the initial vowel systems and before advanced upper-elementary morphology.

The major concepts absorbed into Grade 2 units are early prefix/suffix exposure, consonant-le, open and closed syllables as stand-alone labels, and continuing high-frequency irregular words. Although standards mention common prefixes and suffixes in Grade 2, they are usually functioning here as support for longer-word decoding, not yet as the full morphology units that become more coherent in Grade 3.

## Grade Three through Grade Five

### Grade Three overview

Grade 3 is the clearest transition point from phonics-heavy spelling into formal morphology and conventions. Common Core identifies the most common prefixes and derivational suffixes, common Latin suffixes, multisyllabic words, and irregular words at Grade 3. New York expects common prefixes and suffixes, multisyllabic words, and suffix meaning work. Florida explicitly includes Greek and Latin roots and affixes, common derivational suffixes, and multisyllabic words. Fundations Level 3 describes its focus as “advanced spelling rules and morphology,” and HD Word shifts in its later units to prefixes, suffixes, doubling, and multisyllabic derived words.

| Grade Unit                             | Purpose                                                                                       | Why it belongs in Grade 3                                                                                                     | Prerequisites                                                             | Prepares students for                                                       |
| -------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Prefixes                               | Introduce prefixes as stable meaning-bearing units attached to base words.                    | This is the first grade where affixes consistently function as true morphology rather than merely longer-word supports.       | Multisyllabic-word awareness, base-word reading, inflectional endings.    | Greek/Latin morphology, derived words, academic vocabulary.                 |
| Suffixes                               | Introduce common derivational suffixes as units that change meaning and often part of speech. | Standards and curricula consistently elevate suffixes in Grade 3.                                                             | Inflectional endings, base-word awareness, multisyllabic reading.         | Suffix spelling changes, word families, upper-elementary derivation.        |
| Spelling Changes When Adding Suffixes  | Teach doubling, dropping silent e, changing y to i, and the three sounds of -ed.              | Once suffixes are productive, students need the spelling rules that govern how suffixes attach to base words.                 | Silent e, short-vowel vs. long-vowel contrast, suffix awareness.          | Advanced morphological spelling, derived words, final-stable-syllable work. |
| Plurals, Possessives, and Contractions | Teach common written conventions that depend on accurate endings and apostrophes.             | Grade 3 is the most coherent place to treat these as a grouped conventions unit after inflectional endings are already known. | Inflectional endings, basic noun knowledge, high-frequency word accuracy. | Upper-grade editing, conventional writing, commonly confused words.         |
| Homophones and Commonly Confused Words | Use meaning to choose among spellings that sound alike or look similar.                       | By Grade 3, students know enough phonics that the next barrier is often meaning-based spelling choice.                        | Vowel patterns, high-frequency words, basic sentence-level meaning.       | Upper-elementary usage, proofreading, academic writing accuracy.            |

The recommended ordering is: Prefixes → Suffixes → Spelling Changes When Adding Suffixes → Plurals, Possessives, and Contractions → Homophones and Commonly Confused Words. This sequence works because it moves from visible word parts, to what those parts do, to what happens when they are attached, and then into the writing conventions and meaning-based choices that depend on those skills. Words Their Way’s developmental model also supports this shift from within-word patterns into “syllables and affixes” and then toward derivational relations.

The concepts absorbed into Grade 3 units are base-word awareness, part-of-speech change, and most review of earlier syllable types. Those ideas are important, but they are not better Grade Units than Prefixes, Suffixes, and Spelling Changes When Adding Suffixes. Proofreading transfer continues in Grade 3, but it is still better treated as a strand that runs through these units rather than as a standalone topic.

### Grade Four overview

By Grade 4, standards stop listing many small phonics topics separately and instead expect integrated use of sound-spelling correspondences, syllabication, and morphology. Common Core and New York both expect unfamiliar multisyllabic words to be handled through combined knowledge of letter-sound correspondences, syllable patterns, roots, and affixes. New York Grade 4 also explicitly calls for Greek and Latin affixes and roots as clues to meaning. Florida similarly describes Grade 4 instruction as applying all letter-sound correspondences, syllabication patterns, and morphology, while Grades 4–5 vocab standards highlight Greek and Latin roots and affixes. Texas Grade 5, which continues that upper-elementary band, explicitly names final stable syllables, advanced syllable division, prefixes, suffixes, and suffix-based base-word changes.

| Grade Unit                                     | Purpose                                                                              | Why it belongs in Grade 4                                                                                                      | Prerequisites                                                      | Prepares students for                                                        |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Greek and Latin Roots                          | Introduce roots as recurring meaning-bearing parts in academic words.                | Grade 4 is the strongest consensus point for formal root study as a regular part of word analysis.                             | Prefixes, suffixes, multisyllabic words, meaning-based word study. | Advanced affixes, academic vocabulary, derived words, content-area spelling. |
| Advanced Multisyllabic Words                   | Move beyond simple syllable division into integrated word analysis for longer words. | Upper-elementary standards increasingly expect students to combine syllabication and morphology in unfamiliar words.           | Syllable types, common affixes, vowel-pattern control.             | Final stable syllables, schwa work, derived academic words.                  |
| Final Stable Syllables and Common Word Endings | Teach endings such as -tion, -sion, -ture, and related final-syllable patterns.      | These patterns are common in upper-elementary academic vocabulary and are widely taught once students can manage longer words. | Multisyllabic words, suffixes, hard/soft c and g awareness.        | Derived-word spelling, roots plus endings, upper-grade vocabulary work.      |
| Derived Words and Word Meaning                 | Show how related words preserve spelling and meaning connections across a family.    | This is where students begin to use morphology and meaning together to explain less-transparent spellings.                     | Prefixes, suffixes, roots, homophone awareness.                    | Grade 5 academic word study, conventional spelling, etymological awareness.  |

The best Grade 4 ordering is: Greek and Latin Roots → Advanced Multisyllabic Words → Final Stable Syllables and Common Word Endings → Derived Words and Word Meaning. Roots give students meaningful handles on longer words; advanced multisyllabic work gives them structural control; final stable syllables add a major academic-word pattern set; and derived-word work integrates sound, spelling, and meaning across families. This sequencing is especially consistent with the upper-elementary shift described by Common Core, New York, Texas, LETRS, and Moats.

The main concepts absorbed into Grade 4 rather than separated are schwa, dictionary syllabication/origin work, and hard/soft c and g review. Schwa is real and important, but it is usually best taught inside advanced multisyllabic and final-syllable work rather than as a separate Grade Unit. Hard and soft c and g continue to matter, but by Grade 4 they function more as part of advanced word analysis than as the whole instructional center.

### Grade Five overview

Grade 5 continues upper-elementary spelling, but the work becomes even more integrated. Common Core and New York keep the focus on combined use of letter-sound correspondences, syllabication patterns, and morphology for unfamiliar multisyllabic words. Texas Grade 5 explicitly includes advanced syllable division, final stable syllables, prefixes, suffixes, suffix-driven base-word changes, and Greek and Latin roots such as geo and photo. Florida’s upper-elementary benchmarks likewise emphasize morphology and Greek/Latin affixes and roots. Educationally, that means Grade 5 is better thought of as advanced integrated word study than as a return to many new isolated phonics units.

| Grade Unit                                  | Purpose                                                                                         | Why it belongs in Grade 5                                                                                                      | Prerequisites                                                                  | Prepares students for                                                |
| ------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Advanced Roots, Affixes, and Academic Words | Consolidate roots and affixes inside more complex academic vocabulary.                          | By Grade 5, the curriculum expects students to use morphology productively in content and context.                             | Greek and Latin roots, prefixes, suffixes, advanced multisyllabic words.       | Middle-school vocabulary, disciplinary reading, spelling in writing. |
| Spelling Changes in Related Words           | Teach how spelling shifts across derivations while preserving meaning relationships.            | Upper-elementary learners now have enough morphology to understand changes across related words, not just simple suffix rules. | Suffix changes, roots, derived-word awareness.                                 | Secondary academic vocabulary, etymology, editing.                   |
| Meaning-Based and Conventional Spelling     | Consolidate homophones, commonly confused words, irregular spellings, and usage-driven choices. | At this stage, many remaining difficulties are no longer basic phonics errors but choice, convention, and meaning errors.      | Homophones, high-frequency-word knowledge, morphology, sentence-level meaning. | Middle-school writing accuracy and independent proofreading.         |

The recommended Grade 5 ordering is: Advanced Roots, Affixes, and Academic Words → Spelling Changes in Related Words → Meaning-Based and Conventional Spelling. This is intentionally a smaller set than in earlier grades because Grade 5 is less about many brand-new elementary concepts and more about advanced integration of what has already been built. That also fits the standards language in Common Core, New York, Florida, and Texas, which becomes explicitly integrative in upper elementary.

The concepts absorbed into Grade 5 are proofreading transfer, dictionary/reference skills, and general editing routines. Those are important curriculum outcomes, but they should remain embedded across all upper-elementary spelling units, not elevated into separate Grade Units. Texas TEKS, for example, frames spelling within recursive integrated language strands rather than as a separate isolated block.

## Spiral strands and absorbed concepts

Several topics should be treated as spiral strands rather than as repeated Grade Units. The strongest examples are:

- High-Frequency Words

- dictation and spelling transfer

- review of earlier short- and long-vowel patterns

- multisyllabic word practice

Standards and curricula repeatedly show that earlier knowledge is not abandoned; instead, it is re-used with greater complexity. Texas explicitly says the strands are recursive and that earlier standards are applied with greater depth over time. ILA similarly argues that all threads of spelling knowledge are useful across grades, even if some dominate at particular times.

Several major concepts from the broader taxonomy are absorbed rather than promoted to Grade Units:

- Phonological awareness is absorbed primarily into Kindergarten Sounds, Letters, and Early Encoding and remains embedded in dictation and word-building work afterward.

- Letter formation/handwriting remains foundational support, especially in Kindergarten and Grade 1, but is not a spelling Grade Unit in this blueprint.

- Open and closed syllables are absorbed into Syllables and Two-Syllable Words in Grade 1 and Syllable Types and Multisyllabic Words in Grade 2, because those syllable labels are most useful as organizing tools rather than parent-facing yearlong units.

- Consonant-le, schwa, and many specific subpatterns such as -tch, -dge, -mb, -kn, or oi/oy are absorbed into broader units—especially Syllable Types and Multisyllabic Words, Silent Letters and Ending Spelling Patterns, and Diphthongs and Other Vowel Patterns—so that the curriculum stays stable and intelligible.

- Proofreading transfer, dictionary use, and editing for spelling in writing remain essential outcomes, but they are woven throughout upper elementary rather than repeated as standalone units.

- The biggest area of disagreement across curricula is timing, not the concepts themselves. Digraphs may appear late in kindergarten in some programs and in Grade 1 in others. R-controlled vowels may appear in late Grade 1 or Grade 2. Prefixes and suffixes are exposed in Grade 2 by some standards, but they become full morphology units more often in Grade 3. Greek and Latin roots may appear as early hints in Grade 3, but the stronger consensus point for formal study is Grades 4–5. This blueprint resolves those differences by assigning each concept to the grade of primary educational coherence, not the earliest possible exposure.

## Canonical K–5 grade-unit curriculum

The following is the recommended canonical Grade Unit curriculum for U.S. elementary spelling. It is intentionally neither microscopic nor oversized. Each unit is large enough to sustain multiple practice lists, dictation sets, and subskills, but specific enough that teachers and parents immediately understand what it teaches.

| Grade        | Canonical Grade Units                                                                                                                                                |
| ------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Kindergarten | Sounds, Letters, and Early Encoding; Short Vowels and CVC Words; High-Frequency Words                                                                                |
| Grade 1      | Consonant Digraphs and Blends; Inflectional Endings; Silent E and Long Vowels; Vowel Teams; Syllables and Two-Syllable Words                                         |
| Grade 2      | R-Controlled Vowels; Diphthongs and Other Vowel Patterns; Syllable Types and Multisyllabic Words; Silent Letters and Ending Spelling Patterns; Hard and Soft C and G |
| Grade 3      | Prefixes; Suffixes; Spelling Changes When Adding Suffixes; Plurals, Possessives, and Contractions; Homophones and Commonly Confused Words                            |
| Grade 4      | Greek and Latin Roots; Advanced Multisyllabic Words; Final Stable Syllables and Common Word Endings; Derived Words and Word Meaning                                  |
| Grade 5      | Advanced Roots, Affixes, and Academic Words; Spelling Changes in Related Words; Meaning-Based and Conventional Spelling                                              |

Taken as a whole, this blueprint preserves the main pillars that recur across the research base: alphabetic encoding, one-syllable phonics patterns, larger vowel systems, syllables and multisyllabic words, morphology, high-frequency irregular words, and meaning-based/conventional spelling. It also matches the way high-quality curricula actually behave: the early grades are more granular because new patterns are being established, and upper elementary becomes more synthetic because students are expected to combine orthography, morphology, and meaning in longer academic words.

The strongest recommendation, therefore, is to treat this Grade Unit set as the educational architecture of the website: not as page inventory, not as navigation, and not as a rigid commercial-program clone, but as the canonical grade-level structure from which later skill pages, practice collections, and instructional sequences can be derived. That is the simplest model that still faithfully represents mainstream U.S. elementary spelling instruction.
