### OI and OY Grouped by Syllable Position

| Spelling | Where it appears | Words |
|---|---|---|
| oi | Not at the end of a syllable | oil, coin, point, voice, noise |
| oy | At the end of a syllable | boy, joy, enjoy, royal, loyal |

Reading each row aloud shows the same gliding sound in every word. Only the spelling — and where that spelling sits relative to a syllable boundary — changes.

### What Are the OI and OY Vowel Teams?

OI and OY are two-letter vowel teams that spell a diphthong: a vowel sound that glides from one mouth position to another while it's being said, rather than holding steady the way a plain long or short vowel does. Say *boy* slowly and notice the mouth shape change partway through the sound — that glide is the whole point of the pattern. *Enjoy* has the same glide in its second syllable; *coin* has it in its only syllable.

### Why OY Ends a Syllable and OI Doesn't

In the words taught here, *oy* is used when the sound falls at the end of a syllable — most simply, the end of the whole word, as in *boy*, *joy*, and *enjoy*, but also the end of a syllable inside a longer word, as in *roy-al* and *loy-al*. *Oi* is used everywhere else: inside a syllable, followed by another consonant sound in the same syllable, as in *oil*, *coin*, *point*, *voice*, and *noise*.

Framing this as "syllable-final" rather than "word-final" matters once a learner meets a word like *royal*: the OY isn't at the very end of the word, but it is at the end of the word's first syllable, so the pattern still holds. A learner who only knows "OY goes at the end of the word" will find *royal* confusing; a learner who knows "OY goes at the end of a syllable" won't.

### What Should the Learner Notice?

- OI and OY spell the exact same sound — a gliding vowel, not two different sounds.
- The spelling choice depends on position within a syllable: OY at the end of a syllable, OI elsewhere in it.
- In a short, one-syllable word, "end of a syllable" and "end of the word" are the same thing, which is why *boy* and *coin* look like a simple middle-versus-end split.
- In a longer word like *royal*, the OY still ends a syllable — just not the whole word — so the same reasoning applies without needing a separate rule.

### A Genuine Exception Worth Knowing

A small number of words break the pattern outright — *koi*, the ornamental fish, ends a one-syllable word in plain *oi* rather than *oy*, and a few other borrowed words behave the same way. These are uncommon enough in early reading and writing that they don't need to be taught alongside the regular pattern, but they're worth knowing about so a learner isn't thrown by one later.

### How to Practice OI and OY Words

1. Say the target word aloud and listen for the glide — the sound moving from one mouth position to another.
2. Break the word into syllables if it has more than one.
3. Ask whether the gliding sound is at the end of a syllable (choose *oy*) or somewhere else within one (choose *oi*).
4. Write or type the letters in order, then read the result back exactly as written.

If a learner writes *boy* as *boi* or *coin* as *coyn*, step 3 is the one to return to: say the word again, find the syllable break if there is one, and ask where the sound falls relative to that break. Let the learner work out which spelling fits rather than simply supplying the correct letters.

### Signs the OI/OY Pattern Is Becoming Secure

Signs the pattern is becoming secure include:

- Reading a new OI or OY word aloud with the correct gliding sound.
- Choosing OI or OY correctly for an unfamiliar regular word based on syllable position.
- Spelling a representative word from dictation.
- Explaining, in their own words, why *royal* uses OY even though it isn't at the very end of the word.
