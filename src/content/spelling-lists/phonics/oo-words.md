### The Same Spelling, Two Different Sounds

| Sound | Words |
|---|---|
| Long — as in *moon* | moon, food, zoo, spoon |
| Short — as in *book* | book, look, foot, good |

Read each row aloud: every word in the top row uses one vowel sound, every word in the bottom row uses a different one, and both rows are spelled with the same two letters, *oo*.

### Why OO Isn't Like Most Other Vowel Teams

Most vowel teams taught so far spell one sound, sometimes with a position tendency that hints at the spelling (AI/AY, OI/OY) and sometimes with no reliable pattern at all but still just one sound (OU/OW). OO is different in kind: it genuinely spells two separate vowel sounds, and there's no visual signal on the page — no position, no neighboring letter, no capitalization — that reveals which one a new word uses. A learner has to know the specific word, or be willing to try both sounds and see which one sounds like a real, familiar word.

### A Loose Tendency, Not a Rule

Some phonics guidance points out that *oo* followed by the letter *k* is more often short (*book*, *look*, *took*, *cook*, *hook*) than long. This is true often enough to be worth mentioning, but it isn't reliable enough to teach as a rule a child can lean on — plenty of common *oo* words with no *k* at all are also short (*good*, *foot*), and the tendency says nothing about the many *oo* words that don't involve *k* in the first place. Treat it as a mild hint at most, never as something to apply with confidence.

### What Should the Learner Notice?

- The same two letters, *oo*, spell two different sounds — this isn't a mistake or an exception, it's simply how this vowel team works.
- Neither sound is the "correct" or default one; both are common.
- There's no way to tell which sound a new word uses just by looking at the letters *oo* — the word itself has to be recognized.
- A handful of words, including *roof* and *root*, are genuinely said with either sound depending on the speaker — this is a real feature of spoken English, not a sign the learner heard it wrong.

### How to Practice OO Words

1. Say the target word aloud, trying the long sound (as in *moon*) first.
2. If that doesn't sound like a real, familiar word, try the short sound (as in *book*) instead.
3. Confirm the choice against a word already known that shares the sound.
4. Write or type the letters in order, then read the result back exactly as written.

If a learner reads *foot* with the long sound instead of the short one (or the reverse), don't simply supply the correct pronunciation. Ask whether the word they said is one they recognize, and if not, have them try the other sound — building the habit of checking a guess against real, known words rather than assuming *oo* always sounds the same way.

### Signs the OO Pattern Is Becoming Secure

Signs the pattern is becoming secure include:

- Reading a new *oo* word aloud, trying both sounds if the first guess doesn't sound like a real word.
- Spelling a representative word from dictation for both the long and short sound.
- Explaining, in their own words, that *oo* can spell two different sounds and that neither one is wrong.
- Recognizing that a word like *roof* might be said differently by different people without treating either version as a mistake.
