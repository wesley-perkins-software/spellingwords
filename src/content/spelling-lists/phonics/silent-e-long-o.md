### Long O Word Families

| Family | Words |
|---|---|
| -one | bone, cone, stone |
| -ope | hope, rope, slope |
| -ote | note, vote |
| -oke | broke, joke, poke, smoke |

### What Is the Long O Silent E Pattern?

Long o is the vowel sound that says its own name, as in the middle of *home* and *hope*. By this point in the silent-e sequence, a learner has already seen the same rule at work with two other vowels — long a and long i — so long o is usually where the pattern starts to feel automatic rather than freshly taught each time: a final silent e signals that the o should say its long sound rather than a short one, the same mechanism covered in Long A Silent E Words and Long I Silent E Words.

### Why These Words Matter for Reading

*Home*, *hope*, *note*, and *stone* are among the most frequent words a beginning reader meets, so recognizing this pattern pays off quickly in both reading and writing. The pattern also holds with a taught digraph in front of it, as in *chose* — the *ch* digraph and the silent-e pattern work independently of each other.

### What Should the Learner Notice?

- The silent e still makes no sound of its own; it still changes the vowel two letters back.
- The pattern works the same with a digraph in front, as in *chose* or *those*, or a blend, as in *stone*.
- A short list of very high-frequency words spelled with a silent e after *o* don't follow this pattern at all — see below.

### Long O Exceptions Worth Naming: Gone, Done, Some, and Come

A handful of extremely high-frequency words end in a silent e after *o* but don't say long o. *Gone* keeps a short, open vowel instead of rhyming with *bone* and *cone*. *Done*, *some*, and *come* all say a short u sound instead, as in *cup*. The number word *one* is the most irregular of the group — its spelling doesn't clearly match either its consonant sound or its vowel sound. None of these follow the regular VCe rule, and because they show up constantly in early reading and writing, they're worth teaching directly as high-frequency words rather than left as a surprise a learner discovers on their own.

### Long O Compared With Short O

| Short o | Long o |
|---|---|
| hop | hope |
| not | note |
| rod | rode |

### How to Practice Long O Silent E Words

1. Say the target word aloud.
2. Stretch it into its sounds together, noticing that the final e adds no sound of its own (*h... ō... m*).
3. Write or type the letters in order, including the silent e.
4. Read the result back exactly as written.

If a word comes out wrong, step 4 is the key one: read back exactly what was written — "that says *hom*" — and let the learner listen for what's missing, rather than pointing to the correct letters directly. Hearing the mismatch themselves is what builds the skill; being handed the answer skips it. For the exception words (*gone*, *done*, *some*, *come*, *one*), skip the sound-it-out step entirely and practice them as high-frequency words instead — sounding them out would lead to a wrong answer.

### Signs the Long O Silent E Pattern Is Becoming Secure

Signs the pattern is becoming secure include:

- Hearing long o in a new, unfamiliar word.
- Reading a long-o silent-e word not seen before.
- Spelling a representative word from dictation.
- Recognizing *gone*, *done*, *some*, and *come* on sight rather than trying to sound them out with the regular rule.
- Explaining, in their own words, why *home* isn't spelled *hom*.
