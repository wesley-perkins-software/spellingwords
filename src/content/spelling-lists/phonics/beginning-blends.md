### What Is a Beginning Consonant Blend?

A beginning consonant blend is two or three consonant letters at the start of a word that each keep their own sound, said in quick succession. In *clap*, the /k/ and the /l/ are both heard before the vowel; in *stop*, both /s/ and /t/ are heard. Say either word slowly and notice that nothing is silent and nothing merges — this is what separates a blend from a digraph, where two letters like *ch* or *sh* combine into one brand-new sound with no trace of either original letter's usual sound. This page focuses on common two-letter beginning blends in simple, regular words, not on every consonant pairing English allows.

### Common Beginning Blend Families

Beginning blends fall into three loose groups, based on which letter comes second:

| Blend group | Blends | Example words |
|---|---|---|
| L-blends | bl, cl, fl, gl, pl, sl | clap, flag, plum |
| R-blends | br, cr, dr, fr, gr, pr, tr | grab, crab, drum |
| S-blends | sm, sn, sp, st, sw | stop, swim, spin |

Reading a row aloud and listening for both consonants before the vowel starts is more useful here than memorizing the group names — the groups are just a way to organize practice, not a rule a learner needs to recite.

### What Should the Learner Notice?

- Both consonant sounds are heard, in order, before the vowel begins.
- The blend is still two connected sounds, not one new sound — that's what makes it different from a digraph.
- Swapping the blend at the front of a word (*clap* → *flap* → *slap*) changes the beginning but not the pattern itself: two consonants, then the vowel.
- The same both-sounds-heard idea applies whether the blend has two letters (*st*) or three (*str*).

### Beginning Blends Compared With Beginning Digraphs

A beginning blend and a beginning digraph can look similar on the page — both are two letters at the start of a word — but they behave completely differently out loud:

| Beginning blend | Beginning digraph |
|---|---|
| stop | shop |
| trip | chip |

In *stop*, the /s/ and /t/ are both distinct sounds a learner can stretch apart. In *shop*, the /sh/ is one sound — there's no separate /s/ or /h/ hiding inside it. The same is true of *trip* against *chip*: two sounds versus one. A useful test for either word is simply to ask, "Do I still hear both letters, or just one sound?"

### Common Beginning Blend Mistakes

The most common mistake is dropping the first consonant and spelling only the second — writing *top* for *stop*, or *wim* for *swim*. This usually means the learner heard the vowel and the more prominent second consonant but glossed over the quieter first sound. Slowing the word down and isolating just the blend, before saying the rest of the word, tends to fix this quickly: *s... t... op*, then *stop*.

A second, less frequent slip is treating a beginning blend like a digraph and searching for one "new" sound instead of two — this is more likely for a learner who has just finished practicing digraphs and is still expecting every consonant pair to fuse.

### How to Practice Beginning Blend Words

1. Say the target word aloud.
2. Stretch it into its sounds together, keeping the two blend sounds separate (*c... l... a... p*).
3. Write or type one letter for each sound, in order.
4. Read the result back exactly as written.

If a word comes out wrong, step 4 is the key one: read back exactly what was written — "that says *lap*" — and let the learner listen for which sound is missing, rather than pointing to the correct letter directly. Hearing the gap themselves is what builds the skill; being handed the answer skips it.

### Signs the Beginning Blend Pattern Is Becoming Secure

Signs the pattern is becoming secure include:

- Hearing both consonant sounds in a new, unfamiliar beginning blend.
- Telling a beginning blend apart from a beginning digraph in a spoken word.
- Reading a beginning-blend word not seen before.
- Spelling a representative beginning-blend word from dictation, without dropping the first consonant.
- Explaining, in their own words, why a blend keeps two sounds while a digraph makes one.
