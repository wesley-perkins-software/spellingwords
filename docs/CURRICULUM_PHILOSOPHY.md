# Curriculum Philosophy

## Context

`docs/PRODUCT_VISION.md` defines what spellingwords.app is as a product. `docs/CONTENT_STANDARDS.md` defines what the library is willing to publish. `docs/LIST_SPECIFICATIONS.md` defines how an individual list is planned before it's written. This document sits above all three for one specific question they don't fully answer: **what makes a list a spelling list, as opposed to a vocabulary list that happens to live on a spelling website.**

That distinction was not always applied consistently in the library's history. A curriculum audit found multiple published lists — mostly kindergarten and upper-elementary "content vocabulary" pages — that were built around a topic (shapes, family members, science words) rather than a spelling principle, and did not represent what students are actually assigned to spell at that grade in mainstream U.S. classrooms. This document is the permanent standard that keeps that from recurring, and the record of why the correction mattered enough to remove and replace pages that already had real editorial investment behind them.

---

## What spellingwords.app is

A spelling curriculum: a K–5 sequence of lists, each one teaching a specific spelling skill — a phonics pattern, a morphological rule, an orthographic rule, a canonical high-frequency word set — at the grade where mainstream U.S. elementary instruction actually teaches it. The ambition, per `docs/PRODUCT_VISION.md`, is to be the best spelling website on the internet: the one teachers trust first and parents recommend to each other.

## What it is not

- **Not a vocabulary website.** Knowing what a word means, or being able to recognize and discuss it, is a different skill from being able to spell it. This site teaches the second one.
- **Not a general language-arts resource.** Reading comprehension, grammar, and writing instruction are valuable, but they are not this site's job.
- **Not a thematic educational content library.** A collection of words that share a topic (animals, feelings, shapes, science) is a legitimate thing to build — it is just not a spelling curriculum, and this site does not publish it as one.

## The inclusion test

Every list in the library must answer **yes** to one question:

> Would a mainstream U.S. elementary teacher reasonably assign these specific words for spelling practice at this grade level?

Not: would students recognize these words. Not: would they hear or discuss these words. Not: are these words taught in science, math, or social studies. Only whether a teacher would put this exact word set on a spelling assignment at this grade.

A fast, practical version of the test: **can the list name its shared spelling pattern in one sentence?** ("Short-a CVC words." "Words with the -tion suffix." "Dolch pre-primer sight words.") If the honest answer is "these words share a topic, not a pattern," the list fails the test regardless of how useful, popular, or well-written it is.

## Why spelling curriculum differs from vocabulary curriculum

A spelling lesson is organized around a **transferable pattern** — something a learner can apply to words beyond the ones on the page. A phonics-pattern list teaches a decoding rule; a prefix/suffix list teaches a morphology rule; a Dolch list teaches automatic recognition of the words that recur most often in print. In every case, mastering the list makes the learner better at spelling *other* words too.

A vocabulary/topic list does not do this. Knowing that a kindergartener can spell "circle," "triangle," and "star" doesn't help them spell any other word — there's no shared rule to generalize from. That doesn't make the words unimportant; it makes them the wrong kind of list for a *spelling* site to publish as if they were a graded spelling progression.

## Why educational integrity outranks search demand

This site's own history is the case study. Several kindergarten and grade 4–5 pages (shape words, family words, school words, feelings words, food words, community/civics vocabulary, math vocabulary) had genuine search demand and years of prior editorial investment — readiness signals, FAQ, body copy, learning-progression links, all written to the same bar as the rest of the library. They were removed anyway, once each failed the inclusion test, because:

- **Topical authority is a single-focus asset.** A site that unambiguously means "authentic spelling curriculum" on every page is a stronger long-term candidate to become *the* trusted spelling resource than a hybrid spelling-and-vocabulary site, even if the hybrid site has more total pages.
- **Trust compounds, in both directions.** A teacher who finds one page that isn't really a spelling list has reason to wonder whether other pages are similarly padded. Every accurate page makes the library more trustworthy; every inaccurate one discounts all of them.
- **The prior investment was not wasted.** Writing those pages against the same editorial bar as everything else is exactly what made the gap visible — the process of building them to a high standard is what revealed they were the wrong content for this particular site. Some of their words even resurface later in the curriculum, taught for the right reason instead of the wrong one (for example, *family* has real value as a grade 5 example of an unstressed-syllable spelling pattern — it just isn't a kindergarten spelling word).

When search demand and educational integrity conflict, integrity wins. A page that would attract traffic but doesn't represent authentic spelling instruction does not belong in the library, no matter how small the effort to keep it would be.

## Reference architecture

Grade-level spelling content should progress along this spine, roughly in order of introduction:

CVC/CVCC words → digraphs and consonant blends → silent-e long vowels and vowel teams → r-controlled vowels → inflectional endings (-s, -es, -ed, -ing) and possessives → compound words, contractions, regular plurals → prefixes and suffixes, doubling/dropping/y-to-i spelling rules → multisyllabic words and root families → Greek/Latin roots and upper-elementary derivational patterns (schwa in unstressed syllables, assimilated prefixes, silent/sounded consonant pairs in related words).

A new list proposal should be placed against this spine — "where does this pattern belong in the sequence" — rather than justified by "what topic haven't we covered yet." If a proposed list doesn't map onto any point on this spine, that's a strong signal it's a vocabulary list, not a spelling list.

## Standing rule for future content decisions

Every new list proposal must state its shared spelling pattern and grade justification *before* any words are chosen, using the template in `docs/LIST_SPECIFICATIONS.md` and the vocabulary/duplicate rules in `docs/CONTENT_STANDARDS.md`. This document supplies the test a proposal must pass; those documents supply the worksheet for building it once it does. If a list can't pass the inclusion test above, it does not get written — regardless of search volume, topical relevance, or how much of the curriculum it would appear to "complete."
